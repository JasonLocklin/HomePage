There's a tremendous amount of spilled ink, or rather worn out keyboards, about the enshitification of Windows 11 and having AI shoved into seemingly every application, the "year of the Linux desktop" and the various complaints about how that doesn't work for people because of feature a or feature b. Within Linux, there's the dramatic over-churn of platforms, software installation models, systemd, audio systems, etc. It's enough to take the fun out of computing and just leave people stressed out over which choice is the "best". Best operating system? best distro? best gui toolkit? Give me a break.

The desktop model of computing was pretty much a solved problem 2 decades ago.

What if the goal is "good enough" software and then get things done? It's not any less subjective, or personal, than the "best", but it can be the low-stress way to be content with personal computing. It's an approach to computing that I recently realized has been something I have actually been long using, if not explicitly. When I first started using Linux many years ago, I was a heavy user of Microsoft Word. At the time, LibreOffice's predecessor could replicate pretty much everything, with a few glitches that made passing files sometimes lose some fidelity. How did I deal with that? I shifted to simpler documents that had fewer issues, avoiding tinkering with anything complex, and then producing a final pdf when precise details mattered. It's just a word processor. Who cares if nested tables with wingdings don't convert right - just write your shit. Get your feedback. Print the final product to pdf. The software was good enough.

I've since simplified even further. Now? I need a good text editor or two. Solved problems. An email client. Good enough. Web browser. Commodity. I've actually started tracking what I install on the computer, and paring it down periodically. Do I need this? Is this new, more complicated software actually improving my life, or was the simpler, more long-term stable stuff good enough?

As I have developed this thought, I have found that more and more of my interaction with computing can be plain text files, in a well-organized filesystem hierarchy, operated on with a terminal-based text editor and basic Unix tools to be good enough to do most of the things I want to do. It's quite amazing actually, that this type of environment has been slowly refined over decades to be efficient and capable, without requiring a lot of computer resources or re-training of our brains. And that latter point is where the power of "good enough" takes hold.
